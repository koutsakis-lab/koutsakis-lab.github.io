  <div class="research-detail-top__text">
    <p>
      Aerospace dominance depends on propulsion systems, and heat transfer often limits how efficient
      those devices can be. We study rockets, gas turbines, and rotating detonation engines from the
      wall inward: what the gas does to the surface, how much heat crosses it, and how long the
      material underneath survives.
    </p>
    <p>
      Our work in this area includes:
      <ul>
        <li><ins>Rotating detonation engines:</ins> Operating a hydrogen–air rotating detonation engine as a materials testbed, and measuring what the detonation environment actually does to engine walls.</li>
        <li><ins>Heat flux at the gas–wall interface:</ins> Calorimetric and sensor-based measurement of wall heat flux under detonation and high-frequency combustion conditions.</li>
        <li><ins>Detonation-driven transport:</ins> Understanding where classical conduction models break down when shockwaves, not smooth gradients, set the thermal loading.</li>
        <li><ins>Engine architectures:</ins> Translating measured thermal and mechanical loads into design guidance for chambers, nozzles, and cooled components.</li>
      </ul>
    </p>

    <h3>RDE Materials Testbed</h3>
    <p>
      The world's first academic rotating detonation engine materials testbed, built at UNM to
      expose candidate coatings and wall architectures to realistic detonation environments and
      rank them on the metrics that matter for engine design.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-detail-card">
    <div class="figure-placeholder">Figure coming soon</div>
    <h3>Heat Flux Measurement</h3>
    <p>
      Calorimetric heat flux measurements in a hydrogen–air rotating detonation engine give the
      boundary condition that thermal and structural models of detonation-based engines have
      largely had to assume.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-detail-card">
    <div class="figure-placeholder">Figure coming soon</div>
    <h3>Detonation Shockwaves</h3>
    <p>
      Detonation waves load a wall far faster than diffusion can respond. We examine where
      Fourier's law stops describing the near-wall response, and what that means for predicting
      surface temperature and coating life.
    </p>
