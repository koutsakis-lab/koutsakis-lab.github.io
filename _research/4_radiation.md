  <div class="research-detail-top__figure">
    <img src="/assets/img/research/radiation_coating.png" alt="Schematic of radiative heat transfer through a translucent barrier coating on a cooled metallic substrate, showing emission, reflection, transmission, scattering, re-emission, and conversion to phonons">
    <p class="research-detail-caption"><i>Current barrier coatings are translucent across most of the thermal radiation spectrum. Radiation is absorbed, scattered, re-emitted, and only partly converted to heat where you want it.</i></p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-detail-top__text">
    <p>
      As combustion temperatures rise, an increasing share of the heat load arrives as radiation
      rather than convection. This is a problem for the coatings currently in service: yttria-stabilized
      zirconia and gadolinium zirconate are nearly transparent from roughly 0.5 to 6.5 µm, which is
      exactly where a 2000–2500 K gas does most of its radiating. Thermal conductivity, the property
      those coatings were selected on, does not help.
    </p>
    <p>
      So we treat the coating as an optical component as well as a thermal one, and ask what
      absorption and scattering properties would actually lower the temperature at the
      coating–alloy interface. The same question, inverted, governs fibrous insulation: there the
      goal is to reflect the radiation before it ever enters.
    </p>
    <p>Our work in this area includes:</p>
    <ul>
      <li><ins>Radiation barrier coatings:</ins> Two-flux spectral modeling of combined radiative and conductive transport, identifying the absorption–scattering combinations that reduce interface temperature.</li>
      <li><ins>Spectrally selective absorbers:</ins> Rare-earth doped zirconates for narrow-band absorption, and broadband "black" oxides that absorb across the radiation window.</li>
      <li><ins>Reflective fibrous insulation:</ins> Monte Carlo radiative transfer through fiber mats, and the design metrics that follow from it.</li>
      <li><ins>Design rules from microstructure:</ins> A Kuhn scattering length borrowed from polymer physics, and a fiber size metric that sets the optimal radius directly from the source temperature.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

  <div class="research-detail-card">
    <img src="/assets/img/research/radiation_window.png" alt="Blackbody spectral emissive power at 2000, 2500 and 3000 K plotted against the absorption coefficient of a plasma-sprayed gadolinium zirconate coating, showing a transparent window below about 6 microns">
    <h3>The Optical Gap</h3>
    <p>
      A hot gas radiates almost entirely below 6&nbsp;µm. That is exactly where today's coatings
      barely absorb at all — so the radiation passes through the barrier and into the alloy.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-detail-card">
    <img src="/assets/img/research/fibrous_sem_ct.jpg" alt="Scanning electron micrographs and micro-computed tomography reconstructions of commercial alumina and zirconia fibrous insulation">
    <h3>Real Fiber Mats</h3>
    <p>
      Electron microscopy and micro-CT give the fiber radius distribution and orientation that the
      scattering model needs, and show the non-idealities — curvature, roughness, fragments — that
      the model does not.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="research-detail-card">
    <img src="/assets/img/research/fiber_scattering.png" alt="Schematic of light scattering from a coated cylindrical fiber, showing the incident field and the resulting scattering cone">
    <h3>Scattering from a Fiber</h3>
    <p>
      A fiber scatters into a cone set by the incidence angle, and coated fibers can be collapsed
      onto an effective refractive index. Single-fiber behavior is what the mat reflectance is
      built from.
    </p>
  </div>
